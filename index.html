      🎯 The Verdicts

      Bottom Line
      The single reconciled answer at the very top of the page. It combines the Long-Term Investment Verdict, Valuation Verdict, Risk Level, and Master Verdict into one plain sentence, so you never have to guess what to do when the badges below it disagree with each other.
      How to read it: if a colored badge further down seems to contradict this line, trust this line — it was built specifically to account for that disagreement, not the other way around.
      Master Verdict
      Blends short-term technicals (RSI, MACD, candlestick patterns, breakouts) with fundamentals (ROE, Piotroski, Graham/Lynch gap) into one bull/bear score.
      Best for: "does today's price action and the underlying business both look good right now." Not built specifically for long-term holding — see the Long-Term Investment Verdict for that.
      Long-Term Investment Verdict
      Fundamentals and quality trends only — deliberately ignores RSI, MACD, candlesticks, and every short-term technical signal, so a stock's long-term case isn't diluted by this week's price action.
      Best for: buy-and-hold decisions, not entry timing.
      Risk Level (Low / Medium / High)
      Based on volatility, historical max drawdown, Sharpe ratio, and debt — says nothing about direction.
      How to read it: a "High" risk stock can still carry a "Buy" verdict — Risk Level tells you how much it might swing, not whether to buy it. Use it to decide position size, not whether to act.
      Valuation Verdict (Cheap / Fair / Expensive)
      Based on the Graham/Lynch fair-value gap, PEG ratio, and free cash flow yield — says nothing about business quality.
      How to read it: a stock can be "Cheap" and still be a bad business (cheap for a reason). Always read this alongside the Long-Term Investment Verdict, not on its own.
      NB Score
      A 10-point Piotroski-style checklist built for long-term investors: 3 points from the chart (price above its 200-day average, MACD bullish, no bearish divergence in the last 40 sessions) and 7 from fundamentals (ROE, debt levels, ROIC vs WACC, promoter holding direction, profit growth vs sales growth, price vs Graham Number, Altman Z-Score zone).
      Score = number of checks passed / number of checks that could be evaluated
      How to read it: 8+/10 Excellent, 6-7 Good, 4-5 Average, below 4 Weak. A check that can't be evaluated (missing data) is left out entirely rather than counted as a fail — so the score is always "out of however many checks actually had data," same as Piotroski.

      📈 Technical Indicators

      52-Week Range
      Shows where today's price sits between its lowest and highest close over the past year.
      Position on bar = (Current − Low) / (High − Low) × 100%
      Near the green end = trading close to its 52-week high; near the red end = trading close to its 52-week low. The badge shows exactly how far below (or above) the high the current price is.
      RSI (Relative Strength Index)
      A 0–100 momentum gauge comparing the size of recent gains to recent losses over 14 days.
      RSI = 100 − 100 / (1 + Avg Gain / Avg Loss)
      Above 70 = overbought (momentum stretched, may cool off). Below 30 = oversold (may be due for a bounce). 40–65 is considered a healthy, non-extreme zone.
      MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)
      The gap between a fast (12-day) and slow (26-day) moving average, with its own 9-day "signal line" smoothing that gap.
      MACD = EMA(12) − EMA(26) · Histogram = MACD − Signal
      MACD above its signal line (positive histogram) = bullish momentum; below = bearish.
      Moving Averages (SMA / EMA)
      The average closing price over the last N days — SMA weights every day equally, EMA weights recent days more heavily.
      Price above its 200-day average is a classic long-term uptrend signal; below is a downtrend. A "Golden Cross" (50-day average crossing above the 200-day) is a widely-watched bullish trend-change signal.
      Bollinger Bands
      A band plotted above and below a moving average, sized by recent volatility (standard deviation).
      Price hitting the upper band = statistically stretched to the upside short-term; the lower band = stretched to the downside. Bands squeezing together often precede a sharp move in either direction.
      Ichimoku Cloud
      A cluster of five lines forming a shaded "cloud" that shows trend direction, momentum, and support/resistance all at once.
      Price above the cloud = bullish trend; below = bearish; inside the cloud = no clear trend, treated as a caution zone.
      Fibonacci Retracement
      Horizontal lines (23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%) drawn between a recent swing high and low, based on the observation that pullbacks often stall near these specific fractions of the prior move.
      Used as potential support (in an uptrend pullback) or resistance (in a downtrend bounce) levels — not a guarantee, just a commonly-watched zone.
      Stochastic Oscillator & Williams %R
      Both compare today's close to its recent trading range, similar in spirit to RSI.
      Stochastic above 80, or Williams %R near 0 = overbought. Stochastic below 20, or Williams %R near -100 = oversold.
      Candlestick Patterns & Breakout/Breakdown
      Candlestick patterns (hammer, engulfing, doji, etc.) are specific price-bar shapes traders associate with short-term reversals or continuations. A breakout/breakdown is price moving decisively beyond a recent high/low, often flagged when accompanied by above-average volume.
      Divergence (RSI / MACD)
      When price makes a new high (or low) but RSI or MACD doesn't confirm it with a new high (or low) of its own.
      Bearish divergence (price up, momentum down) is an early warning a rally may be losing steam. Bullish divergence is the mirror case during a decline.

      💰 Valuation Models

      P/E, P/B, and PEG Ratio
      P/E = Price / EPS · P/B = Price / Book Value per Share · PEG = P/E / Growth Rate
      P/E is "how many years of current profit you're paying for." PEG adjusts for growth — a high P/E can still be reasonable if growth is high enough; PEG below 1 is the classic "cheap relative to growth" threshold.
      Graham Number & Graham Formula
      Two fair-value formulas from Benjamin Graham (Warren Buffett's teacher).
      Graham Number = √(22.5 × EPS × Book Value)
      Graham Formula = EPS × (8.5 + 2 × Growth Rate)
      Trading below either suggests a margin of safety. A negative growth rate is floored at 5% for the Formula version, since the formula breaks down with negative inputs — that floor is noted whenever it's used.
      Peter Lynch Fair Value
      Fair Value = EPS × Growth Rate
      Lynch's rule of thumb: a fairly-priced growth stock's P/E should roughly equal its earnings growth rate. Trading well below this suggests the market hasn't priced in the growth yet.
      Acquirer's Multiple & Magic Formula Earnings Yield
      Deep-value tools popularized by Joel Greenblatt and Tobias Carlisle.
      Acquirer's Multiple = Enterprise Value / EBIT · Magic Formula Yield = EBIT / Enterprise Value
      Lower Acquirer's Multiple = cheaper on an operating-earnings basis; under 6× is the classic deep-value/takeover-candidate threshold. Magic Formula Yield is just its inverse, shown as a percentage.

      🏦 Quality & Financial Health

      ROE, ROCE & ROIC
      All three measure how efficiently a company turns capital into profit — they just use slightly different denominators (Shareholder Equity, Capital Employed, or Invested Capital).
      Higher, and more importantly consistent over several years, is better. A single great year means less than a steady 15%+ over 5 years.
      DuPont Analysis
      Breaks ROE down into three drivers to show where the return is actually coming from.
      ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
      ROE driven mainly by margin and turnover is higher quality than ROE driven mainly by leverage (a high Equity Multiplier) — leverage-driven ROE is more fragile in a downturn.
      Piotroski F-Score
      A 9-point accounting-quality checklist across profitability, leverage/liquidity, and operating efficiency — 1 point for each rule satisfied.
      8–9 = financially sound; 0–2 = weak fundamentals. Built to separate genuinely strong value stocks from statistically-cheap-but-weak ones.
      Altman Z-Score
      A bankruptcy-risk formula combining five balance-sheet and income-statement ratios.
      Above 2.99 = Safe Zone; 1.81–2.99 = Grey Zone; below 1.81 = Distress Zone. Designed for non-financial companies — not meaningful for banks, NBFCs, or insurers, whose balance sheets work completely differently. This app's version approximates one input (working capital) since Screener's standard view doesn't report it directly — treat it as indicative, not precise.
      ROIC vs. WACC
      Is the company earning more on its capital (ROIC) than that capital actually costs to raise (WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital)?
      ROIC > WACC means the business is genuinely creating shareholder value with each rupee reinvested; ROIC < WACC means it's effectively destroying value even if profits are growing.
      Rule of 40
      Rule of 40 Score = Revenue Growth % + Profit Margin %
      A score of 40+ suggests a growth business is scaling in a healthy balance — not sacrificing all profitability just to grow revenue, or vice versa.
      Debt-to-Equity
      Debt-to-Equity = Total Borrowings / Shareholder Equity
      Lower = a more conservative balance sheet with less risk in a downturn or rate spike. Above 1.0-1.5x generally warrants a closer look at why.

      ⚠️ Risk Metrics

      Sharpe Ratio
      Sharpe = (Annual Return − Risk-Free Rate) / Annual Volatility
      Return earned per unit of total risk taken. Higher is better; negative means the stock hasn't even compensated for the risk-free rate.
      Sortino Ratio
      Like Sharpe, but only penalizes downside volatility — a stock that occasionally jumps sharply upward isn't penalized for that "risk" the way Sharpe would.
      A Sortino noticeably higher than the Sharpe for the same stock means most of its volatility has been to the upside.
      Beta & Correlation (vs. Nifty 50)
      How much a stock tends to move relative to the overall market.
      Beta > 1 = more volatile than the Nifty 50; Beta < 1 = less. Correlation shows how closely it tends to move in the same direction as the market, separate from how big those moves are.
      Max Drawdown
      The worst peak-to-trough decline in the stock's price history over the period shown.
      A real-world answer to "how bad could this actually get" — more grounded than volatility alone, since it shows an actual historical worst case.
